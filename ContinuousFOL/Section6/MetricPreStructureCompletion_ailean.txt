**Definition.** $(M,d^{\mathfrak M})$ is a pseudo-metric space; let $\hat M$ be its completion and $\iota:M\to\hat M$ the canonical map.

**Lemma.** $f^{\mathfrak M}$ and $P^{\mathfrak M}$ are uniformly continuous on $M^n$. Hence they have unique uniformly continuous extensions $\hat f:\hat M^n\to\hat M$ and $\hat P:\hat M^n\to\mathbb R$ with $\hat f(\iota\bar a)=\iota f(\bar a)$ and $\hat P(\iota\bar a)=P(\bar a)$, using that $\hat M^n$ is the completion of $M^n$.

*Proof.* By Remark 5.8, for every $\epsilon$ there is a single $\delta$ such that tuples within $\delta$ in every coordinate have images within $\epsilon$; this is uniform continuity for the max-distance on $M^n$. A uniformly continuous map from a dense subspace into a complete space (here $\hat M$, respectively $\mathbb R$) extends uniquely to a uniformly continuous map on the completion. The extension agrees with the original map on the image of $\iota$.

**Lemma.** $\hat P$ takes values in $[0,1]$; the extended metric symbol $\hat d$ coincides with the metric of $\hat M$; and $\hat f,\hat P$ satisfy the same one-argument moduli $\delta_{s,i}$ as $f,P$.

*Proof.* Each property is a closed condition that holds on the dense image of $M$ and is preserved under limits. For the moduli: if $d(c,e)<\delta(\epsilon)$ in $\hat M$, approximate $c,e$ and the other arguments by points of $M$ that are still $\delta(\epsilon)$-close (possible because the inequality is strict); the modulus inequality holds for the approximants and passes to the limit by continuity.

**Definition** (`metricCompletion`). $\widehat{\mathfrak M}=(\hat M,\hat f,\hat P)$ is a continuous $\mathcal L$-structure: $\hat d$ is the complete metric of $\hat M$, and the moduli hold.

**Lemma.** $\iota$ is an $\mathcal L$-morphism with dense image.

*Proof.* The identities $\hat f(\iota\bar a)=\iota f(\bar a)$ and $\hat P(\iota\bar a)=P(\bar a)$ of the extension lemma are exactly the morphism conditions. Density of the image is a basic property of the completion.

**Lemma.** If a real function $g$ on a space is continuous with values in $[0,1]$ and $S$ is dense, then $\sup g[S]=\sup g$.

*Proof.* Both suprema exist because the values are bounded by $1$, and $\sup g[S]\le\sup g$ since $g[S]$ is a subset. Conversely, fix a point $b$ and $\epsilon>0$. The set $\{c: g(c)>g(b)-\epsilon\}$ is open because $g$ is continuous, and it contains $b$. So it meets the dense set $S$, which gives $g(b)-\epsilon<\sup g[S]$. Hence $g(b)\le\sup g[S]$ for all $b$.

**Theorem** (`metricCompletionElementary`). $\iota$ is elementary: $\mathfrak M(\varphi,\sigma)=\widehat{\mathfrak M}(\varphi,\iota\circ\sigma)$.

*Proof.* Induction on $\varphi$; atomic formulae and connectives are as for morphisms. For $\sup_x\alpha$: the map $b\mapsto\widehat{\mathfrak M}(\alpha,(\iota\circ\sigma)^b_x)$ is continuous on $\hat M$ (by Theorem 6.8 applied in $\widehat{\mathfrak M}$), its values at $b=\iota(a)$ are $\mathfrak M(\alpha,\sigma^a_x)$ by induction, and $\iota[M]$ is dense; so the supremum over $\hat M$ equals the supremum over $M$.
